OSS Gravity Map — Top 50 Engineers

55,851 engineers across 29 projects, measured by EIS

We pointed the Git Telescope at 29 of the world's most influential open-source projects — React, Kubernetes, Terraform, Redis, Rust, ClickHouse, and 23 others — and measured 55,851 engineers.

The first thing the data showed is who still holds up each universe today: Jose Valim (Phoenix), Donny/kdy1 (swc), Evan You (Vite), Ritchie Vink (Polars), Alexey Milovidov (ClickHouse), Taylor Otwell (Laravel). These names lead their projects' gravity — but note that almost none of them saturate it. Gravity here is a strict conjunctive gate: it credits structure only where the code still survives under other people's pressure AND others have built on it. A founder whose code was rewritten over the years, or who works a quiet corner no one else touches, decays toward the floor no matter how famous. That is why the leaderboard numbers are modest, and why a name being near the top means more than it used to.

The more revealing finding is what the gate does to the famous. Some of the most celebrated names in OSS are no longer the gravitational center of the code they started. Rust is the clearest case. Graydon Hoare started the language and Niko Matsakis designed its borrow checker — its conceptual backbone — yet under this gate neither tops Rust's gravity today. A decade of others rewriting and extending their foundation means their lines no longer carry the load; the current load-bearers (Nicholas Nethercote, Ralf Jung, Michael Goulet) do. The telescope is not disrespecting the founders — it reports, honestly, that structural dependence has moved. Gravity tracks the code that is still there, not the credit for having written the first version. React tells a gentler version: Sebastian Markbåge still leads React's gravity, but only at 9.4 — high catalysis (others build on him), little of his own code surviving under churn.

The other side of the same gate is the 250 Hidden Architects it surfaces. They don't give conference talks. They don't have mass followings. Many don't appear in the official maintainer lists. Yet when we trace the gravitational field lines, there they are — their code still surviving where others actively work, others still building on it. Mark Raasveldt (DuckDB) and Josh Goldberg (eslint) hold structural gravity well above engineers far more famous. The gate doesn't reward who arrived first; it reports whose code the system still leans on.

And then there are the Entropy Fighters — 38 engineers across 29 projects who show strong signals on both Debt Cleanup and Survival. They are the people who wake up and choose to fight the second law of thermodynamics in code. Without them, every project would eventually collapse under its own weight.

To the founders whose gravity has moved on: that is not a demotion — it is the system having absorbed your design so thoroughly that others now carry it. To the hidden ones holding it up today: the mass of your contribution was invisible until now, but it was never unfelt. Every developer who builds on your still-living code owes a piece of their productivity to the structure you keep load-bearing.

This map is for you.

How this ranking works — and what it does not claim.

Engineers are ranked by Gravity, a structural shape (0.45·Design + 0.25·Breadth + 0.30·Indispensability) passed through two necessary gates: catGate = 0.15 + 0.85·Catalysis/100 and survGate = 0.15 + 0.85·RobustSurvival/100, so Gravity = catGate × survGate × shape. Both gates are necessary: code that survives only in a quiet corner (no one else's pressure) OR that nobody builds on collapses toward the floor. Local gravity is a relative signal within each repository; this table ranks by the cross-universe adjusted gravity, which scales local gravity by ln(universe_size)/ln(max).

Jose Valim's local Gravity 85 in Phoenix and Alexey Milovidov's 15.5 in ClickHouse are observations from different universes — Phoenix has ~1,400 contributors, ClickHouse ~2,600 — and the gate is harder to clear in a larger, more-churned codebase. This is Engineering Relativity: the same engineer produces different gravity in different codebases.

Because the gate is conjunctive, a high number is hard-won: it requires durable code under others' pressure and a foundation others extend, at the same time. The ranking captures whose code the universe still structurally leans on, not who shipped the most or arrived first.

Think of it as mapping the brightest star in each galaxy. Some galaxies are larger than others, but in every galaxy, the star that shapes the gravitational field is observable.
